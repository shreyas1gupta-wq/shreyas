# Cycle Theory: The Canon, the Evidence, and What Survives Contact with Data

This is the theoretical foundation for the cycle stack. Its purpose is not to celebrate
cycle theory but to sort it: which cycles have a mechanism, which have evidence, which
have only a story — and therefore how much money each is allowed to move.

The organising principle throughout: **a cycle's allowed influence is bounded by the
number of independent observations of it that exist.** A 4-year inventory cycle has ~15
observations in Indian data and can be estimated. A 60-year long wave has two
observations globally and cannot be estimated at all — it can only be reasoned about.
Both may appear in the model; they may not have the same authority.

---

## 1. The classical cycle schema

Schumpeter's *Business Cycles* (1939) proposed that economic fluctuation is the
superposition of three cycles of different length, each with its own mechanism. That
three-layer schema is still the most useful organising frame available, and the modern
literature has added a fourth (the financial cycle) which is arguably the most
important of all for our purposes.

| Cycle | Period | Mechanism | Evidence tier | Obs. in India |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Kitchin** | 3–5 y | Inventory adjustment; the lag between order and delivery causes over- and under-shooting | B — real but weak and irregular in modern economies | ~7 |
| **Juglar** | 7–11 y | Fixed capital investment; capacity is built in lumps, over-built, then digested | B — the classic "business cycle", visible but irregular | ~3 |
| **Kuznets** | 15–25 y | Infrastructure and building; demographic and migration-driven construction waves | C/B — contested, but the property cycle is real | ~1.5 |
| **Kondratiev** | 45–60 y | Technological revolution and its diffusion; credit and price long waves | C — two to three observations, heavily contested | <1 |
| **Financial cycle** | 16–20 y | Credit and property price co-movement; leverage build-up and deleveraging | **A/B — the strongest modern evidence of the four** | ~1.5 |

### 1.1 Kitchin (1923)

Kitchin identified a 3–5 year cycle in commodity prices, interest rates and inventories.
The mechanism — firms mis-estimate demand, accumulate unwanted inventory, cut production
to clear it, then over-cut — is real and still visible in manufacturing PMIs and in
sector-level inventory data.

**Why it matters less than it used to.** Just-in-time manufacturing, better demand
signalling, and the shift of developed economies toward services have all damped the
inventory cycle. In India it remains more visible than in the US because manufacturing
and commodities are a larger share of the listed market, and because agricultural and
monsoon-linked inventory swings add a genuinely periodic component.

**How we use it.** As a short-horizon input to sector rotation (cement, steel, chemicals,
autos), not as a market-level timing signal. Detectable in free data: IIP by use-based
category, sector-level inventory-to-sales from quarterly filings, capacity utilisation
from the RBI OBICUS survey.

### 1.2 Juglar (1862)

The original business cycle. Juglar's insight was that crises are not accidents but the
recurring phase of a cycle driven by fixed investment. Capacity is added in response to
high margins, the addition takes years, by the time it arrives demand has shifted, margins
compress, investment stops, capacity is digested, and the cycle restarts.

India's capex cycle is a textbook Juglar: the 2003–2008 boom, the 2009–2013 hangover with
stalled projects and stressed balance sheets, the 2014–2019 digestion and NPA cleanup, and
the 2021 onward upturn. That is roughly one and a half cycles of clean observation.

**How we use it.** Capacity utilisation, new project announcements, gross fixed capital
formation as a share of GDP, and corporate profit-to-GDP together define a capex-cycle
phase. This drives the industrials/capital-goods sector tilt and the value-vs-quality
factor tilt.

### 1.3 Kuznets (1930) and the property cycle

Kuznets found 15–25 year swings in construction and infrastructure, tied to demographic
waves and migration. Modern work is skeptical of Kuznets as a *general* macroeconomic
cycle, but the **property cycle** specifically is well documented, and Homer Hoyt's and
later Fred Harrison's work on the roughly 18-year land cycle has an uncomfortable
predictive record.

India's residential cycle: the 2003–2008 boom, the 2013–2020 seven-year downturn with
enormous unsold inventory, and the 2021 onward recovery. One clean observation.

**How we use it.** As a sector input (real estate, cement, housing finance, building
materials) and as a component of the household balance-sheet and credit-cycle picture.
Free data exists: the RBI House Price Index and the RBI Residential Asset Price Monitoring
Survey.

### 1.4 Kondratiev (1925) and the long-wave problem

Kondratiev's long waves of 45–60 years in prices and production are the most famous and
the least defensible of the classical cycles. The mechanism proposed — clusters of
technological innovation, or long credit and price waves — is plausible. The evidence is
not.

**The core problem is arithmetic.** With reliable data from roughly 1790 for the UK and
1870 for most economies, a 55-year cycle gives at most four observations, of which perhaps
two are clean. No statistical procedure can identify a cycle from two observations. Solomou
(1998) examined the long-wave claim carefully across countries and found the evidence
does not support a regular Kondratiev cycle; what exists is a set of long swings of
irregular length that are better explained by specific historical events (wars, the gold
standard, oil shocks) than by an endogenous cycle.

**Our position.** Kondratiev-scale reasoning enters the model as a *Bayesian prior on the
regime*, never as a timing signal, and is capped at a small influence. It is tier C. We
state its phase as a wide distribution and we let it move the strategic centre of gravity
by a few percentage points at most — never more.

### 1.5 The financial cycle — the one that actually matters

The most important development in cycle research since 2008 is the identification of the
**financial cycle**: a medium-term cycle in credit and property prices, longer than the
business cycle at roughly 16–20 years, larger in amplitude, and far more consequential
because its downturns coincide with banking crises.

The key results, and they are unusually robust:

- **Borio (2014)** synthesises the finding that the financial cycle has a period roughly
  twice the business cycle, and that recessions coinciding with financial-cycle downturns
  are far deeper and longer than ordinary recessions.
- **Drehmann, Borio & Tsatsaronis (2012)** measure it via credit, the credit-to-GDP ratio
  and property prices, and show these three co-move at the medium-term frequency.
- **Schularick & Taylor (2012)**, using 14 countries over 1870–2008, show that **credit
  growth is the single best predictor of financial crises** — a result that survives
  extensive robustness testing and is the strongest quantitative claim in the whole cycle
  literature.
- **Jordà, Schularick & Taylor (2013), "When Credit Bites Back"** show that recessions
  preceded by credit booms are systematically deeper and slower to recover.
- **Aikman, Haldane & Nelson (2015)** confirm the credit cycle is distinct from the
  business cycle and operates at a lower frequency.

**Why this is the anchor of our long/mid-cycle layer.** It has the best evidence, a clear
mechanism, an established measurement (the credit-to-GDP gap, published free by the BIS
for India), and a documented asymmetric payoff — the cost of ignoring a late-stage credit
boom is a 40–60% drawdown. For a strategy whose binding constraint is *drawdown below the
Nifty 50's*, this is the single most valuable signal in the entire stack.

India's own credit cycle is legible: the 2004–2008 boom, the post-crisis stimulus surge,
the 2013–2019 twin balance-sheet crisis and NPA cleanup, the 2018 IL&FS/NBFC freeze, and
the post-2021 clean-balance-sheet expansion. That is roughly one and a half cycles — enough
to reason with, not enough to fit a model to.

## 2. Instability and reflexivity: why cycles exist at all

Cycles need a mechanism, or they are numerology. Three frameworks supply one.

### 2.1 Minsky's financial instability hypothesis

Minsky's claim is that **stability is destabilising**. In a long expansion, borrowers and
lenders progressively relax standards because the recent past has been benign. Financing
structures migrate through three stages:

1. **Hedge finance** — cash flows cover interest and principal.
2. **Speculative finance** — cash flows cover interest but principal must be rolled.
3. **Ponzi finance** — cash flows cover neither; the position depends entirely on asset
   price appreciation.

The share of Ponzi units rises endogenously through the expansion, and the system becomes
fragile not through an external shock but through its own success. The "Minsky moment" is
when asset prices stop rising and Ponzi units are forced to liquidate.

**This is the mechanism underneath the financial cycle**, and it is why the credit cycle
is asymmetric — slow build, fast collapse. Our credit-cycle phase classifier is
essentially an attempt to measure the migration through Minsky's three stages, using
observable proxies: interest coverage ratios across the corporate universe, the share of
firms with interest coverage below 1, debt-funded dividend and buyback activity, covenant
quality, and the spread between marginal and average borrowing cost.

### 2.2 Soros's reflexivity

Soros's argument is that in markets, participants' beliefs alter the fundamentals they are
trying to assess — prices are not merely a passive reflection of fundamentals but a causal
input to them. A rising share price lowers the cost of equity, enables acquisitions, funds
capex, and improves reported growth, which justifies the higher price.

This matters for us because it explains why **valuation and momentum can both work and can
point in opposite directions for years**. The boom/bust sequence Soros describes — an
underlying trend, a prevailing bias that reinforces it, a period of acceleration, a moment
of truth, a twilight period, and a crossover into decline — maps directly onto how our
euphoria score should behave: momentum-confirming in the middle of a trend and contrarian
only at the extremes.

### 2.3 Austrian business cycle theory

Mises and Hayek argued that credit expansion below the natural rate of interest distorts
the structure of production toward long-dated, capital-intensive projects that are not
justified by real savings, and that the bust is the necessary liquidation of those
malinvestments.

The framework is not empirically testable in its strong form and modern macroeconomics
largely rejects it. But it contributes one genuinely useful and measurable idea: **the real
policy rate relative to a neutral rate is a leading indicator of malinvestment**, and
periods of deeply negative real rates should raise our prior on subsequent capital
misallocation. We use it that way and no further.

## 3. The very long wave: currency, debt and hegemony

This is the 100–250 year layer the owner explicitly wants. Intellectual honesty requires
stating up front: **there is no statistical evidence for a cycle at this frequency and
there cannot be.** What exists is a set of historical case studies with a common structure.

### 3.1 The structure that recurs

Dalio's *Principles for Navigating Big Debt Crises* (2018) and *Principles for Dealing with
the Changing World Order* (2021) synthesise the pattern across the Dutch, British and
American reserve-currency periods and their predecessors:

1. A rising power accumulates competitiveness, savings and eventually reserve-currency
   status.
2. Reserve status permits cheap borrowing, which is used.
3. Debt accumulates faster than income over decades.
4. The debt service burden becomes politically unpayable through austerity.
5. The debt is devalued — through inflation, financial repression (nominal rates held
   below inflation), or explicit restructuring.
6. Internal wealth-gap conflict and external great-power conflict intensify.
7. A new monetary order emerges.

Reinhart & Rogoff's *This Time Is Different* (2009) supplies the empirical backbone across
eight centuries, and their central finding is directly usable: **sovereign debt crises,
banking crises, inflation crises and currency crashes cluster together**, and the sequence
typically runs banking crisis → sovereign debt crisis → inflation/currency crisis.

### 3.2 What is actually measurable

The value of this layer is not prediction; it is that it tells us **which tail to hedge**.
The measurable state variables, all available free:

| Indicator | Source | What it tells us |
|---|---|---|
| Global and US debt-to-GDP | BIS, IMF | Position in the long debt cycle |
| Real policy rate vs. history | FRED, RBI | Whether financial repression is active |
| USD share of allocated FX reserves | IMF COFER (quarterly, free) | Reserve-currency erosion |
| Central bank net gold purchases | World Gold Council | Reserve diversification, the clearest current signal |
| Gold vs. CPI and vs. M2 | FRED, RBI | Monetary debasement pricing |
| India external debt / GDP, import cover | RBI DBIE | India's own external vulnerability |
| INR REER deviation from trend | RBI (40-currency and 6-currency indices) | Currency over/undervaluation |

### 3.3 The discipline this layer requires

Because it cannot be validated statistically, this layer is governed by hard rules rather
than by fit:

- Its phase is a **wide distribution**, never a point estimate.
- It may move the strategic allocation by a **small, capped** amount — single-digit
  percentage points.
- It may change its state **at most once every several years** (a minimum dwell time), and
  hysteresis bands are wide.
- It expresses itself primarily through **gold weight and tail-hedge intensity**, which are
  the positions whose payoff profile matches a low-probability, high-impact regime change.
- Its "phase slide" is explicit: named observable triggers (a sovereign downgrade, a
  currency crisis, a reserve-system change, a debt monetisation episode) may advance or
  delay the assumed phase by a bounded number of years. Nothing else moves it.

## 4. Cycle theories we deliberately exclude

Intellectual hygiene requires naming what we reject and why.

| Excluded | Why |
|---|---|
| **Elliott Wave** | Unfalsifiable — the wave count is revised after the fact to fit any price path. No prior specification means no test. |
| **Gann angles, harmonics, planetary cycles** | No mechanism; the apparent fits are curve-fitting over a large space of possible periodicities. |
| **Fixed-period calendar cycles** (decennial patterns, the "seven-year cycle") | Sample sizes of 10–15 non-independent observations; fails any multiple-testing correction. |
| **Strauss–Howe generational theory** | Interesting as social history, unfalsifiable as a forecasting tool, and the periods are fitted retrospectively. |
| **Benner cycle and similar 19th-century schedules** | Survivorship of a single lucky series; no out-of-sample record. |

The exclusion test is simple and applied uniformly: **can the cycle's phase be specified
in advance, in writing, in a way that could be wrong?** If not, it does not enter the model.

## 5. From theory to influence budget

The practical output of this document is the mapping from evidence quality to allowed
authority.

| Tier | Definition | Estimation | Max influence (aggressive) | Max influence (moderate) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **A** | ≥10 independent observations; established mechanism; replicated cross-country | Fit from data with proper CV | Up to 15 pp per signal | Up to 10 pp |
| **B** | 2–10 observations; clear mechanism; cross-country support | Prior-anchored, shrunk heavily toward neutral | Up to 8 pp | Up to 5 pp |
| **C** | <2 observations; mechanism plausible; narrative evidence | Fixed rules, no fitting, wide bands | Up to 3 pp | Up to 2 pp |

Cross-country evidence is the key device that upgrades a tier. A cycle observable only 1.5
times in India may have 15 observations across the Jordà–Schularick–Taylor panel of 18
advanced economies since 1870. **Using other countries' histories as pseudo-observations
for India's long cycles is the single most useful methodological trick available to us**,
and it is what makes the credit-cycle layer estimable at all. It is developed in
`02-ECONOMETRIC-METHODS.md`.

## 6. The aliasing problem

Ten cycle signals do not mean ten independent bets. The credit cycle, the business cycle,
the earnings cycle, the NPA cycle, the capex cycle and the profit-to-GDP cycle are largely
**one underlying factor observed six ways**. Stacking them naively produces a bet six times
larger than intended, which is precisely how a levered book breaches a drawdown limit.

The model handles this with an explicit hierarchy: signals are grouped into families, a
single composite is extracted per family (first principal component or a theory-weighted
average), and only the composites enter the allocator. Within-family detail is retained for
attribution and diagnosis but carries no independent allocation authority. The
orthogonalisation order is fixed in advance by horizon — slowest first — so that faster
signals are residualised against slower ones rather than the reverse.
